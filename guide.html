How to read this.

What each number means, where it comes from, and — more usefully — what it cannot tell you.

Prices

Why euro cents, when I pay in kroner

Because the alternative is a lie. A Norwegian krone and a Swedish krona are different units, and a German price quoted in €/MWh is not a Norwegian one quoted in øre/kWh. Laying those on a single axis would invent a difference that isn't there — so everything is converted to euro cents per kWh, which is what the power actually cleared in. Nordic zones keep their own currency in the right-hand column.

What the bar shows

The span from the cheapest to the dearest hour of the day in that zone. The tick inside it is today's average. A long bar means a volatile day, which is worth as much as a high average: it's where the cheap hours live.

What these prices are not

They exclude grid rent, taxes and VAT. Your bill is higher than anything here, often two to three times higher in Norway once nettleie and elavgift are added. What's shown is the part that moves, which is the part worth watching.

Why Iceland is missing

It has no day-ahead market. The grid is isolated, with no interconnector to anywhere, and power is sold on long-term bilateral contracts — mostly to aluminium smelters. There is no spot price to publish. That's a fact about Iceland, not a source we failed to find.

Hydro

Why the gap matters more than the level

A reservoir at 52% tells you nothing on its own. It could be an ordinary autumn or the reason next February is brutal. What makes it legible is the median for the same week of the year across every year since 1995 — so the bars show the gap, not the level. NO2 at 52% against a normal of 84% is a thirty-point hole, and that is the number that will still be being paid for in winter.

Why it's the closest thing here to a forecast

Nordic power is mostly water, and water in a reservoir is energy that has already arrived and hasn't been spent. Everything else on the page describes the present or the past. Storage describes what there is left to sell.

The grid

Flows, and why they explain the price map

A zone is expensive when it is exporting into a dearer neighbour, and cheap when its power has nowhere to go. NO2 runs down the cables to Germany and England and is priced against them; NO4 sits behind constrained transmission in the north. That single mechanism explains most of what the price strip shows.

Direction, not sign

Statnett names each link with a fixed orientation and signs the value, so power running the other way arrives as NO1 → NO5, −1985 MW. True, and unreadable. Direction belongs in the arrow, so the flows here are flipped to always run the way the electricity does.

Weather

Heating demand, not temperature

Degree-hours below 15 °C over the next 48 hours. Below roughly that point buildings start drawing heat, and the size of the gap is what drives demand — not the reading itself. Two zones can sit at the same temperature now and have very different demand coming, which is exactly the case this column is built to show.

Why this is the only leading indicator here

Prices on this page already cleared. Reservoirs were read last week. Weather is the one thing that moves before the market does: cold raises demand and wind raises supply, both ahead of anyone pricing them.

Fuels and currency

Range position

The thin bar under each card: where today's price sits between its six-month low and high. A price can fall on the day and still be near the top of everywhere it has been, and the daily percentage alone will never tell you that.

Why the exchange rates are here

Nordic power clears in euros and is paid for in kroner. So a bill can rise with nothing whatever happening to electricity. Separating the two is the same discipline as splitting a trading profit from a currency move — if you don't, you will eventually congratulate yourself for a weak krone.

At the pump

This is not the price in Oslo

It is not the price anywhere. Duty and VAT are national, so there is no geography in the figure at all, and it excludes retail margin — the one component that genuinely varies by location. Rural stations run dearer, Oslo is competitive. It also ignores the weekly cycle, where prices jump early in the week and drift down toward Sunday. Closer to "what a litre costs before anyone sells it to you" than to any forecourt.

So why model it at all

Because the split is the useful part. Tax is fixed between budgets, so everything that moves is either the refined product or the krone — and a pump price can rise with nothing happening to oil whatsoever. A single number at the roadside merges those two stories into one.

The observed figure underneath

SSB table 09654, the official national average, monthly and about six weeks behind. It cannot tell you today's price. What it can tell you is whether the model has drifted, which is the only reason to trust the model at all. Petrol currently lands within about 0.15 kr of it.

Where this is weakest

The product benchmark is American. RBOB is US Gulf Coast gasoline and heating oil is US distillate, while European fuel prices off Rotterdam and ICE Gasoil. They track closely most of the time, but the transatlantic diesel spread blew out badly in 2022 — exactly the sort of period where this would drift without looking broken. The diesel line carries more of that risk than the petrol one, which is roughly the direction the gap to SSB points.

What this page can't do

It describes a market; it does not predict one. Reservoir gaps and weather lean on the price, but neither determines it — an outage, a policy change or a cold snap in Germany can overwhelm both in a day.

Nothing here is advice about what to buy, sell or fix a tariff on. It is a set of public measurements, assembled so the relationships between them are visible.

Every source is free and keyless: hvakosterstrommen.no, elprisetjustnu.se, energy-charts, Elexon, NVE, Statnett, met.no, Yahoo Finance and Google News. No account, no quota, nothing to expire — which is the only way a page like this is still working in a year.
